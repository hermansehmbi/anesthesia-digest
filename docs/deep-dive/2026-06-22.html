BJAIF 9.2
Withholding or continuing glucose-lowering drugs for elective surgery in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus: a secondary analysis of the MOPED international, prospective, observational study
Donal J. Buggy; Malachy O. Columb; Jeroen Hermanides; Markus W. Hollmann et al. · BJA · 2026-07-01
Bottom line

Continuing metformin on surgery day was associated with a marginal 0.47-day increase in days alive at home by day 30, but the clinical significance is unclear; no benefit or harm found for SGLT2i or GLP-1 RA due to small sample sizes.

🔬 What they did

The authors examined whether withholding or continuing glucose-lowering drugs before elective surgery improves outcomes in patients with type 2 diabetes. They analyzed a prospective international cohort to compare continuation versus discontinuation of metformin, SGLT2 inhibitors (SGLT2i), and glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RA) on the day of surgery.

📐 Design

Secondary analysis of a prospective, observational study conducted across 21 European countries and 89 centres from January 2021 to December 2024. The primary outcome was days alive at home and out of hospital at 30 days (DAH-30). The study enrolled 5767 patients with type 2 diabetes, of whom 4988 (87%) underwent elective surgery.

📊 What they found

Among 3623 participants taking metformin, 421 (12%) continued it on surgery day and had a median DAH-30 of 28 days (IQR 24-29), versus 27 days (IQR 23-29) in those who withheld it (P=0.001). After adjusting for prespecified covariates, continuing metformin remained associated with a higher DAH-30 by 0.47 days (95% CI 0.01-0.93; P=0.044). For SGLT2i (n=836) and GLP-1 RA (n=304), no association was found between continuation or discontinuation and DAH-30. The authors concluded that the sample sizes for newer agents were too small to draw meaningful conclusions.

💡 What it means

The metformin finding shows a statistically significant but clinically marginal benefit of 0.47 days—less than half a day—in time spent at home, which may not justify changing routine preoperative instructions. For SGLT2i and GLP-1 RA, the study is underpowered to guide practice; current guidelines based on mechanism and safety should remain the standard until larger evidence emerges. Most anesthesiologists will continue to follow institutional protocols and perioperative glucose management guidelines rather than alter drug timing based on this small absolute difference.

⭐ Why it matters

Preoperative glucose-lowering drug management remains inconsistent across institutions and guidelines, particularly for newer agents. This study addresses a real bedside question but emphasizes that evidence for withholding versus continuing these drugs during the perioperative period is still limited, especially for SGLT2i and GLP-1 RA.

⚠️ Limitations

This is an observational study without randomized assignment, so confounding cannot be excluded; the metformin association may reflect differences in patient selection rather than the drug itself. The very small sample of SGLT2i and GLP-1 RA users (n=836 and n=304 respectively) limits any inference about these increasingly common agents. The abstract does not report perioperative complications, hypoglycemic events, or other safety outcomes, so the clinical risk-benefit profile remains incomplete.

AnesthesiologyIF 9.1
Pain Trajectories after Orthopedic Surgery in Older Adults: An Analysis of Predictors and Associations with Postoperative Outcomes
Yan Li; Hong Hong; Jia-Hui Ma; Dong-Xin Wang · Anesthesiology · 2026-06-11
Bottom line

Older orthopedic patients show four distinct postoperative pain trajectories; preoperative analgesic use increases risk of unfavorable pain patterns (OR ~3.8), while dexmedetomidine in PCA reduces rapid-decrease trajectory risk (OR 0.47).

🔬 What they did

This secondary analysis examined 710 patients aged 65–90 years undergoing orthopedic surgery to identify distinct pain trajectory patterns and determine which baseline and perioperative factors predicted trajectory membership and postoperative complications. Pain was assessed twice daily for 5 days using an 11-point numeric rating scale.

📐 Design

Secondary analysis of 710 older orthopedic surgery patients using growth mixture modeling to identify pain trajectories and multinomial logistic regression to identify predictors. Complications were tracked through 30 days postoperatively and quality of life assessed at 30 days. The main outcome was classification into one of four distinct pain intensity trajectories. Study setting and specific outcomes not reported in abstract.

📊 What they found

Four pain trajectories emerged: slow decrease (57.9% of patients), rapid decrease (23.4%), increase-decrease (10.8%), and slow increase (7.9%). Preoperative analgesic use significantly increased odds of increase-decrease trajectory (adjusted OR 3.76, P=0.004) and slow increase trajectory (adjusted OR 3.89, P=0.005) compared to slow decrease. Conversely, adding dexmedetomidine to patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) reduced odds of rapid decrease trajectory (adjusted OR 0.47, P<0.001). Specific pain trajectories were associated with adverse postoperative outcomes, though the abstract does not detail which trajectories predicted which complications.

💡 What it means

Older patients entering surgery already on analgesics face higher risk of unfavorable pain control patterns—either worsening or persistently high pain—suggesting that preoperative chronic pain may predict worse postoperative pain dynamics. Adding dexmedetomidine to PCA appears protective against rapid pain resolution, an unexpected finding that may warrant direct review of the full paper to understand mechanistic interpretation. Identifying high-risk patients preoperatively (chronic pain users) could prompt more intensive multimodal analgesia strategies, while dexmedetomidine dosing in PCA may need individualization based on desired trajectory.

⭐ Why it matters

Pain trajectory heterogeneity in older orthopedic patients is clinically relevant because a one-size-fits-all analgesic approach misses subgroups at risk for prolonged or worsening pain and associated complications. Identifying modifiable perioperative predictors (like dexmedetomidine use) offers concrete opportunities to refine pain management in a vulnerable population.

⚠️ Limitations

This is a secondary analysis with no reported details on primary study design, setting, or mechanism of outcome ascertainment, limiting generalizability assessment. The abstract does not specify which postoperative outcomes (e.g., mobility, infection, thromboembolism) were adversely affected by which trajectories, so clinical relevance of trajectory classification to specific complications remains unclear.

EJAIF 6.8
The analgesic efficacy of intrathecal morphine compared to peripheral regional analgesia in total hip arthroplasty
Neel Desai; Eric Albrecht; Sina Grape · EJA · 2026-06-18
Bottom line

Intrathecal morphine and peripheral regional analgesia (nerve block or infiltration) show no difference in pain at rest at 24 hours post-hip arthroplasty, but intrathecal morphine reduces opioid consumption by ~11 mg IV morphine equivalent at cost of more pruritus.

🔬 What they did

This systematic review and meta-analysis asked whether intrathecal morphine or peripheral regional analgesia (peripheral nerve block or local infiltration) provides better pain relief after elective total hip arthroplasty. The review included eight randomized controlled trials enrolling 471 patients undergoing hip replacement under general or spinal anesthesia.

📐 Design

Systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials. Eight trials with 471 patients total were included; peripheral regional analgesia was peripheral nerve block in six trials and local infiltration analgesia in two trials. Primary outcomes were pain score at rest at 24 hours and cumulative IV morphine equivalent consumption at 24 hours.

📊 What they found

At 24 hours, pain at rest did not differ between intrathecal morphine and peripheral regional analgesia (moderate quality evidence). However, intrathecal morphine reduced cumulative IV opioid consumption by a mean of 11.38 mg morphine equivalent (95% CI 4.31–18.45; P = 0.002), though evidence quality was low. At 8–12 hours, intrathecal morphine produced lower pain scores at rest (1.24 point advantage) and on movement (1.15 point advantage), but peripheral regional analgesia significantly reduced in-hospital pruritus (rate reduced by 69%, P = 0.0002 with high quality evidence). No differences in functional status were demonstrated.

💡 What it means

For pain control at 24 hours post-hip arthroplasty, intrathecal morphine and regional techniques are equivalent—choice can be individualized. Intrathecal morphine does reduce systemic opioid use (by about 11 mg), which may be meaningful in opioid-sensitive patients, but this comes at the cost of substantially more pruritus, requiring antihistamines or low-dose naloxone. Neither technique achieves opioid-free analgesia. The advantage of intrathecal morphine narrows after 12 hours, making peripheral techniques attractive if pruritus risk is high.

⭐ Why it matters

Hip arthroplasty patients remain a significant pain management challenge. This head-to-head comparison clarifies that no single modality dominates across all outcomes, so selection should balance opioid-sparing goals against pruritus tolerability and local block feasibility in individual patients.

⚠️ Limitations

Evidence quality for the primary 24-hour pain outcome is only moderate, and quality is low for opioid consumption findings. The abstract does not report detailed trial characteristics, risk of bias assessment, or patient factors (age, comorbidity, baseline pain) that might affect generalizability. Results represent a heterogeneous pool of regional techniques, so findings may not apply equally to all peripheral nerve block types or infiltration approaches.

JCAIF 5.1
Comparison of a gamified versus standard virtual reality simulator to teach ultrasound-guided regional Anaesthesia: A randomised controlled trial
Thomas Vernier; Michelle Chong; Joanne Tan; Jerome Gardiner et al. · JCA · 2026-06-01
Bottom line

Gamification of a virtual reality ultrasound-guided regional anesthesia simulator did not improve learner performance or skill acquisition compared to the standard non-gamified version (p = 0.14 for primary outcome).

🔬 What they did

Researchers compared a gamified virtual reality simulator (with star rewards, achievement milestones, leaderboards, and graphical feedback) against a standard non-gamified simulator for teaching ultrasound-guided regional anesthesia. Sixty novices in regional anesthesia across three Australian hospitals trained over two sessions with deliberate feedback on needling performance.

📐 Design

Prospective single-blinded randomized controlled trial with 60 participants. Primary outcome was improvement in expert-assessed Global Rating Scale. Secondary outcomes included cumulative error scores, computer-generated performance scores, learning curves, cognitive task load (NASA-Task Load Index), and participant satisfaction. Study setting and specific power calculation not reported in abstract.

📊 What they found

The gamified simulator showed no statistically significant difference in the primary outcome—Global Rating Scale improvement—compared to standard simulation after covariate adjustment (F(1,47) = 2.3, p = 0.14). Learning curve gradients did not differ between groups (p = 0.15). Computer-generated performance scores were equivalent (p = 0.51), as were cumulative error scores (p = 0.89) and cognitive task load (gamified −3.5±12.3 versus standard −7.7±11.2, p = 0.17). Both groups found virtual reality simulation beneficial, with 72–92% of participants agreeing or strongly agreeing on 7 of 8 questionnaire items.

💡 What it means

Gamification elements—stars, leaderboards, and milestone rewards—do not enhance skill acquisition in ultrasound-guided regional anesthesia training beyond a well-designed standard simulator. For training programs, investing in gamified platforms for this skill set offers no measurable advantage over traditional structured simulation with adequate feedback. The substantial majority satisfaction in both groups suggests that deliberate practice and expert feedback are the true drivers of learning, not game mechanics.

⭐ Why it matters

With limited training budgets and time, anesthesia educators should prioritize evidence-based simulator features (structured feedback, deliberate practice) rather than assuming gamification automatically improves education. This finding challenges the assumption that game design elements universally enhance medical skill learning.

⚠️ Limitations

The study recruited only novices at three Australian hospitals, limiting generalizability to experienced practitioners or other training environments. The abstract does not report sample size justification, attrition rates, or whether blinding of assessors was maintained. Results are specific to ultrasound-guided regional anesthesia and may not apply to other procedural skills.

ACCPMIF 4.7
Rapid sequence intubation in high-risk patients: what clinicians and researchers must know – a narrative review
Nancy M. Boulos; Patrick Schoettker; Amelie Delaporte; Yonathan Freund et al. · ACCPM · 2026-07-01
Bottom line

RSI remains standard airway practice, but emerging evidence suggests it does not significantly reduce aspiration risk and may increase hypoxemia and hemodynamic instability; success now depends on flexible, patient-centered preparation rather than rigid protocol.

🔬 What they did

This narrative review synthesizes current evidence on rapid sequence intubation (RSI) in critically ill patients across operating rooms, ICUs, and emergency departments. The authors examined how RSI practice has evolved from its original goal of minimizing time to airway protection while preventing aspiration, to a more nuanced approach that balances aspiration prevention against desaturation and hemodynamic complications.

📐 Design

Narrative review of RSI literature and practice; not a primary empirical study. Study design, patient population size, and specific comparator interventions are not reported in the abstract. The review focuses on synthesis of current evidence regarding pharmacological strategies, airway techniques, and human factors in RSI.

📊 What they found

The abstract reports no specific numerical outcomes from trials. However, it presents a key conceptual finding: emerging evidence suggests RSI may not significantly reduce aspiration risk and may actually be associated with increased rates of hypoxemia and hemodynamic instability. The review identifies several interventions with clinically significant reduction of adverse events: video laryngoscope use, apneic bag-mask ventilation, and non-invasive ventilation for pre-oxygenation. Recommended medications include sedatives (etomidate or ketamine preferred over propofol in hemodynamically unstable patients) and paralytics (rocuronium or succinylcholine). First-pass intubation success, patient positioning, apneic oxygenation, and gentle ventilation when appropriate are emphasized as key elements.

💡 What it means

At the bedside, move away from viewing RSI as a rigid, aspiration-focused protocol. Instead, individualize your approach based on patient hemodynamics and oxygenation status. Pre-oxygenate aggressively using non-invasive ventilation or apneic bag-mask ventilation; use video laryngoscopy to maximize first-pass success. In hemodynamically unstable or critically ill patients, choose etomidate or ketamine over propofol. Ensure strong team communication and preparation before the intubation attempt. When appropriate (and patient factors permit), gentle bag-mask ventilation during induction may be safer than strict avoidance of ventilation.

⭐ Why it matters

RSI is performed dozens of times daily across anesthesia practices, yet the traditional aspiration-prevention rationale is being challenged by emerging data. Pivoting from protocol-driven to patient-centered RSI reduces preventable complications like hypoxemia and cardiovascular collapse in high-risk populations—outcomes that directly affect morbidity and mortality in operating rooms, ICUs, and emergency departments.

⚠️ Limitations

This is a narrative review based on abstract-only access; specific trial data, effect sizes, and numerical outcomes are not available. The review synthesizes existing literature but does not report its own primary data. Applicability to specific patient subgroups (e.g., obesity, pregnancy, emergency vs. elective settings) is not detailed in the abstract.

A&AIF 3.8
Dexmedetomidine Preemptive Analgesia on Intra- and Postoperative Pain in Noncardiac Surgery: A Randomized, Double-Blind, Controlled Study
Xiaohua Wang; Haosheng Chang; Wenwei Qi; Hongmei Wang et al. · A&A · 2026-05-28
Bottom line

Preemptive dexmedetomidine (1.5 µg·kg−1·h−1) started 15 minutes before anesthesia induction dramatically reduced postoperative moderate-to-severe pain (2.8% vs 25.9% at 0 hours) and improved intraoperative nociceptive control by NOX monitoring, with opioid-sparing benefit and favorable safety.

🔬 What they did

This study tested whether giving dexmedetomidine before anesthesia induction (preemptive analgesia) could prevent pain sensitization and reduce perioperative pain in noncardiac surgery patients. Researchers enrolled 220 eligible patients and randomized them to receive either intravenous dexmedetomidine 1.5 µg·kg−1·h−1 or placebo (normal saline) from 15 minutes before induction through 15 minutes post-induction.

📐 Design

Single-center, double-blind, randomized controlled trial with 220 patients (108 dexmedetomidine, 112 placebo). The co-primary outcomes were intraoperative NOX compliance rate (mean NOX ≤60 during nociceptive stimulation) and incidence of moderate-to-severe postoperative pain (pain numeric rating score >3) at 0, 24, 48 hours, and 7 days. Secondary outcomes included hemodynamics, postoperative nausea and vomiting, rescue opioid consumption, and safety.

📊 What they found

Dexmedetomidine achieved 90.7% NOX compliance versus 12.5% in placebo (P <0.001). Moderate-to-severe postoperative pain was dramatically lower in the dexmedetomidine group at all time points: 2.8% versus 25.9% at 0 hours, 5.6% versus 29.5% at 24 hours, 0.9% versus 17% at 48 hours, and 0% versus 5.4% at 7 days (P = 0.000). Rescue opioid consumption was reduced in the dexmedetomidine group (z = −2.428, P = 0.004). Modified postoperative nausea and vomiting scores were significantly lower with dexmedetomidine (z = −4.901, P = 0.001). Hemodynamic stability improved, and safety profiles were favorable in the dexmedetomidine group.

💡 What it means

For noncardiac surgery patients, giving dexmedetomidine preemptively reduces clinically meaningful postoperative pain and decreases opioid requirement—a genuinely significant finding. The 23% absolute reduction in moderate-to-severe pain at 0 hours, sustained over 7 days, translates to fewer patients in pain and less rescue analgesia needed. The reduction in PONV is a bonus. This suggests preemptive dexmedetomidine should be considered as part of multimodal analgesia, particularly when opioid-sparing is a goal or when patients are at high risk for severe postoperative pain.

⭐ Why it matters

Over half of the 230 million major surgeries annually have inadequate pain control. A simple, non-opioid intervention given at induction that cuts moderate-to-severe pain rates by nearly 20 percentage points and reduces opioid use addresses a major perioperative quality gap. NOX-guided monitoring adds objective verification that nociception is being controlled.

⚠️ Limitations

This is a single-center study with abstract-only data; full methods, inclusion/exclusion criteria, and surgery types are not reported, so generalizability beyond the studied population is unclear. No information on baseline characteristics, power calculation, or whether results hold across different surgical procedures or patient populations.

RAPMIF 3.5OPEN ACCESS
Addition of liposomal bupivacaine in brachial plexus blocks: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Shing, C. H., Wang, F., Lam, P. M., Tsui, O. W. K., Chan, T. C. W., Wong, S. S. C. · RAPM · 2026-06-05
Bottom line

Liposomal bupivacaine in brachial plexus block reduces 24-hour resting pain by approximately 1 point on a 0–10 scale compared to standard bupivacaine, but the evidence quality is low and clinical benefit may be modest.

🔬 What they did

The authors conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to determine whether adding liposomal bupivacaine to brachial plexus blocks improves postoperative pain control after upper limb surgery. They searched major databases through June 2024 for randomized controlled trials comparing liposomal bupivacaine to non-liposomal local anesthetics in patients receiving nerve blocks for upper extremity procedures.

📐 Design

Systematic review and meta-analysis of 21 randomized controlled trials enrolling 1478 participants. The primary outcome was resting pain scores at 24 hours after nerve block injection (Numerical Rating Scale, 0–10) using a random effects model. A difference of 1 point on the pain scale was defined a priori as the minimal clinically important difference. Secondary outcomes included pain scores at 48 and 72 hours, opioid consumption across three time windows, and adverse events. The GRADE framework was used to assess evidence quality.

📊 What they found

Liposomal bupivacaine reduced resting pain at 24 hours by 1.04 points (95% CI: −1.54 to −0.53, p<0.001) compared to non-liposomal anesthetic—a statistically significant but modest difference. Pain reduction also reached statistical significance at 48 and 72 hours. Opioid consumption was significantly lower at 0–24 hours, 25–48 hours, and 49–72 hours in the liposomal group (p<0.05 for all). Nausea incidence was lower with liposomal bupivacaine. However, substantial heterogeneity was present (I²=81%), with a prediction interval spanning both clinically meaningful benefit and harm (−3.05 to 0.98), and the GRADE rating for the primary outcome was low.

💡 What it means

The 1-point pain reduction matches the threshold you and the authors defined for minimal clinical importance, but the wide prediction interval and low evidence quality mean results vary substantially across studies. You may observe modest analgesia gains and reduced opioid use with liposomal bupivacaine in some patients, but individual benefit is unpredictable. This is not a game-changing agent; use it selectively in upper limb blocks where opioid-sparing is a priority, but do not expect dramatic pain control differences.

⭐ Why it matters

Upper limb surgery often drives opioid consumption in the immediate postoperative period. Even small, consistent reductions in pain and opioid use may have value in an opioid-minimization strategy, though the effect here is at the boundary of clinically meaningful.

⚠️ Limitations

Evidence quality is low, with substantial heterogeneity across included trials, suggesting differences in patient populations, block techniques, or surgical procedures. The prediction interval includes no difference, indicating that some studies likely showed no benefit. Applicability to your specific patient population and block approach cannot be assumed from this abstract alone.

Anaesthetic management of obstetric patients with Chiari 1 malformation with or without syringomyelia: a multidisciplinary consensus statement from the Obstetric Anaesthetists’ Association (OAA)
Yavor Metodiev; Andrew Brodbelt; Natasha Kennedy; Helen Johnston et al. · IJOA · 2026-08-01
Bottom line

An OAA multidisciplinary consensus identified 8 recommendations and 10 statements (low-to-moderate certainty) supporting routine obstetric anaesthetic protocols, including neuraxial techniques, in stable/asymptomatic Chiari 1 patients—but evidence is minimal and guidance does not extend to severely symptomatic disease.

🔬 What they did

The Obstetric Anaesthetists' Association convened a multidisciplinary expert panel to evaluate published evidence and reach consensus on safe anaesthetic management of obstetric patients with Chiari malformation with or without syringomyelia. The panel reviewed antenatal care, mode of delivery, labour analgesia, caesarean anaesthesia, and neuraxial complications in this population.

📐 Design

Structured narrative review of literature. Initial search identified 1187 articles; 49 were deemed relevant and included in the consensus document. Recommendations and statements were graded using US Preventive Services Task Force methodology. Specific comparators and outcome definitions are not reported in the abstract.

📊 What they found

The expert group produced 8 recommendations and 10 statements, all assigned low or moderate level of certainty. The review found almost no published evidence on peripartum anaesthetic management of obstetric patients with significant or rare Chiari symptoms. Consequently, the panel's guidance applies only to patients on the less severe spectrum of the disease—those with stable or no symptoms. No event rates, complication frequencies, or safety statistics are provided in the abstract.

💡 What it means

For obstetric patients with asymptomatic or stable Chiari 1 malformation (with or without syringomyelia), most can be managed using standard obstetric anaesthetic protocols after careful assessment. This implicitly supports consideration of neuraxial analgesia and anaesthesia in appropriate cases, rather than blanket avoidance. However, patients with active neurological symptoms or unstable disease require individualized, case-by-case evaluation—likely involving neurosurgical input—as the consensus provides no evidence-based guidance for that cohort.

⭐ Why it matters

Chiari malformation is encountered sporadically on labour wards but generates anxiety and variable institutional practices because robust evidence is lacking. This consensus aims to reduce unnecessary restrictions and guide safer decision-making in the majority of cases (asymptomatic/stable patients) whilst acknowledging uncertainty in rare, symptomatic presentations.

⚠️ Limitations

The evidence base is extremely limited—only 49 of 1187 screened articles were included, and the abstract notes almost no published evidence exists for symptomatic disease. All recommendations carry low-to-moderate certainty. The consensus excludes severely symptomatic or unstable patients, so findings do not apply to that higher-risk group. Specific thresholds for 'stable' and details of the eight recommendations are not available in the abstract.
