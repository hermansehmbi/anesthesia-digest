BJAIF 9.2
Patient Blood Management in postpartum haemorrhage: a narrative review and expert opinion towards improving patient outcomes during pregnancy and childbirth
Marc Van de Velde; Jan Bláha; Sarah F. Bell; Emilia Guasch et al. · BJA · 2026-07-01
Bottom line

Patient Blood Management (PBM) principles—optimising, managing, and preserving a patient's own blood—improve outcomes in postpartum haemorrhage; the authors provide practical algorithms and reference tools to facilitate PBM uptake in obstetrics.

🔬 What they did

This narrative review synthesised PBM principles and recent postpartum haemorrhage management guidelines, drawing on clinical experience from high-resource European settings. The authors developed an algorithm for managing anaemia in pregnancy and created a quick reference chart for PBM strategies applicable to postpartum haemorrhage scenarios.

📐 Design

Narrative review and expert opinion. The study is not a randomised trial or prospective cohort study. Study size, comparator groups, and formal statistical analysis are not reported. The work synthesises existing guidelines and clinical perspectives rather than presenting original research data.

📊 What they found

No specific outcome statistics or effect sizes are reported in the abstract. The authors state that PBM application is 'associated with improvements in patient outcomes' but do not quantify the magnitude of benefit, event rates, or comparative efficacy versus standard care. The abstract emphasises the clinical need for PBM implementation in obstetrics and identifies key challenges to adoption but does not present numerical results. The main deliverables are the algorithm for anaemia management in pregnancy and the quick reference chart for PBM in postpartum haemorrhage.

💡 What it means

Clinically, this work translates PBM principles into practical obstetric tools that anaesthesiologists can implement at the bedside. The algorithm and reference chart are designed to lower barriers to adoption by providing straightforward guidance on when and how to optimise preoperative haemoglobin, manage fluid and blood products, and preserve the patient's own blood during and after delivery. For practitioners in high-resource settings, these tools offer a structured pathway to incorporate PBM into routine practice for pregnant patients at risk of or presenting with postpartum haemorrhage.

⭐ Why it matters

Postpartum haemorrhage remains a leading cause of maternal morbidity and mortality. Implementing PBM strategies—such as preoperative anaemia correction and judicious transfusion—has the potential to reduce both allogeneic blood exposure and adverse maternal outcomes, yet uptake in obstetrics lags behind other specialties.

⚠️ Limitations

This is a narrative review and expert opinion, not a controlled trial; no quantitative outcomes or effect sizes are provided, making it impossible to assess the magnitude of benefit or the strength of evidence underlying specific recommendations. The perspectives and algorithms are grounded in high-resource European centres and may have limited applicability in low-resource settings or regions with different obstetric infrastructure and blood product availability.

AnesthesiologyIF 9.1
Pain Trajectories after Orthopedic Surgery in Older Adults: An Analysis of Predictors and Associations with Postoperative Outcomes
Yan Li; Hong Hong; Jia-Hui Ma; Dong-Xin Wang · Anesthesiology · 2026-06-11
Bottom line

Older orthopedic patients show four distinct postoperative pain trajectories; preoperative analgesic use increases risk of unfavorable pain patterns (OR ~3.8), while dexmedetomidine in PCA reduces rapid-decrease trajectory risk (OR 0.47).

🔬 What they did

This secondary analysis examined 710 patients aged 65–90 years undergoing orthopedic surgery to identify distinct pain trajectory patterns and determine which baseline and perioperative factors predicted trajectory membership and postoperative complications. Pain was assessed twice daily for 5 days using an 11-point numeric rating scale.

📐 Design

Secondary analysis of 710 older orthopedic surgery patients using growth mixture modeling to identify pain trajectories and multinomial logistic regression to identify predictors. Complications were tracked through 30 days postoperatively and quality of life assessed at 30 days. The main outcome was classification into one of four distinct pain intensity trajectories. Study setting and specific outcomes not reported in abstract.

📊 What they found

Four pain trajectories emerged: slow decrease (57.9% of patients), rapid decrease (23.4%), increase-decrease (10.8%), and slow increase (7.9%). Preoperative analgesic use significantly increased odds of increase-decrease trajectory (adjusted OR 3.76, P=0.004) and slow increase trajectory (adjusted OR 3.89, P=0.005) compared to slow decrease. Conversely, adding dexmedetomidine to patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) reduced odds of rapid decrease trajectory (adjusted OR 0.47, P<0.001). Specific pain trajectories were associated with adverse postoperative outcomes, though the abstract does not detail which trajectories predicted which complications.

💡 What it means

Older patients entering surgery already on analgesics face higher risk of unfavorable pain control patterns—either worsening or persistently high pain—suggesting that preoperative chronic pain may predict worse postoperative pain dynamics. Adding dexmedetomidine to PCA appears protective against rapid pain resolution, an unexpected finding that may warrant direct review of the full paper to understand mechanistic interpretation. Identifying high-risk patients preoperatively (chronic pain users) could prompt more intensive multimodal analgesia strategies, while dexmedetomidine dosing in PCA may need individualization based on desired trajectory.

⭐ Why it matters

Pain trajectory heterogeneity in older orthopedic patients is clinically relevant because a one-size-fits-all analgesic approach misses subgroups at risk for prolonged or worsening pain and associated complications. Identifying modifiable perioperative predictors (like dexmedetomidine use) offers concrete opportunities to refine pain management in a vulnerable population.

⚠️ Limitations

This is a secondary analysis with no reported details on primary study design, setting, or mechanism of outcome ascertainment, limiting generalizability assessment. The abstract does not specify which postoperative outcomes (e.g., mobility, infection, thromboembolism) were adversely affected by which trajectories, so clinical relevance of trajectory classification to specific complications remains unclear.

EJAIF 6.8
The analgesic efficacy of intrathecal morphine compared to peripheral regional analgesia in total hip arthroplasty
Neel Desai; Eric Albrecht; Sina Grape · EJA · 2026-06-18
Bottom line

Intrathecal morphine and peripheral regional analgesia (nerve block or infiltration) show no difference in pain at rest at 24 hours post-hip arthroplasty, but intrathecal morphine reduces opioid consumption by ~11 mg IV morphine equivalent at cost of more pruritus.

🔬 What they did

This systematic review and meta-analysis asked whether intrathecal morphine or peripheral regional analgesia (peripheral nerve block or local infiltration) provides better pain relief after elective total hip arthroplasty. The review included eight randomized controlled trials enrolling 471 patients undergoing hip replacement under general or spinal anesthesia.

📐 Design

Systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials. Eight trials with 471 patients total were included; peripheral regional analgesia was peripheral nerve block in six trials and local infiltration analgesia in two trials. Primary outcomes were pain score at rest at 24 hours and cumulative IV morphine equivalent consumption at 24 hours.

📊 What they found

At 24 hours, pain at rest did not differ between intrathecal morphine and peripheral regional analgesia (moderate quality evidence). However, intrathecal morphine reduced cumulative IV opioid consumption by a mean of 11.38 mg morphine equivalent (95% CI 4.31–18.45; P = 0.002), though evidence quality was low. At 8–12 hours, intrathecal morphine produced lower pain scores at rest (1.24 point advantage) and on movement (1.15 point advantage), but peripheral regional analgesia significantly reduced in-hospital pruritus (rate reduced by 69%, P = 0.0002 with high quality evidence). No differences in functional status were demonstrated.

💡 What it means

For pain control at 24 hours post-hip arthroplasty, intrathecal morphine and regional techniques are equivalent—choice can be individualized. Intrathecal morphine does reduce systemic opioid use (by about 11 mg), which may be meaningful in opioid-sensitive patients, but this comes at the cost of substantially more pruritus, requiring antihistamines or low-dose naloxone. Neither technique achieves opioid-free analgesia. The advantage of intrathecal morphine narrows after 12 hours, making peripheral techniques attractive if pruritus risk is high.

⭐ Why it matters

Hip arthroplasty patients remain a significant pain management challenge. This head-to-head comparison clarifies that no single modality dominates across all outcomes, so selection should balance opioid-sparing goals against pruritus tolerability and local block feasibility in individual patients.

⚠️ Limitations

Evidence quality for the primary 24-hour pain outcome is only moderate, and quality is low for opioid consumption findings. The abstract does not report detailed trial characteristics, risk of bias assessment, or patient factors (age, comorbidity, baseline pain) that might affect generalizability. Results represent a heterogeneous pool of regional techniques, so findings may not apply equally to all peripheral nerve block types or infiltration approaches.

JCAIF 5.1
Comparison of a gamified versus standard virtual reality simulator to teach ultrasound-guided regional Anaesthesia: A randomised controlled trial
Thomas Vernier; Michelle Chong; Joanne Tan; Jerome Gardiner et al. · JCA · 2026-06-01
Bottom line

Gamification of a virtual reality ultrasound-guided regional anesthesia simulator did not improve learner performance or skill acquisition compared to the standard non-gamified version (p = 0.14 for primary outcome).

🔬 What they did

Researchers compared a gamified virtual reality simulator (with star rewards, achievement milestones, leaderboards, and graphical feedback) against a standard non-gamified simulator for teaching ultrasound-guided regional anesthesia. Sixty novices in regional anesthesia across three Australian hospitals trained over two sessions with deliberate feedback on needling performance.

📐 Design

Prospective single-blinded randomized controlled trial with 60 participants. Primary outcome was improvement in expert-assessed Global Rating Scale. Secondary outcomes included cumulative error scores, computer-generated performance scores, learning curves, cognitive task load (NASA-Task Load Index), and participant satisfaction. Study setting and specific power calculation not reported in abstract.

📊 What they found

The gamified simulator showed no statistically significant difference in the primary outcome—Global Rating Scale improvement—compared to standard simulation after covariate adjustment (F(1,47) = 2.3, p = 0.14). Learning curve gradients did not differ between groups (p = 0.15). Computer-generated performance scores were equivalent (p = 0.51), as were cumulative error scores (p = 0.89) and cognitive task load (gamified −3.5±12.3 versus standard −7.7±11.2, p = 0.17). Both groups found virtual reality simulation beneficial, with 72–92% of participants agreeing or strongly agreeing on 7 of 8 questionnaire items.

💡 What it means

Gamification elements—stars, leaderboards, and milestone rewards—do not enhance skill acquisition in ultrasound-guided regional anesthesia training beyond a well-designed standard simulator. For training programs, investing in gamified platforms for this skill set offers no measurable advantage over traditional structured simulation with adequate feedback. The substantial majority satisfaction in both groups suggests that deliberate practice and expert feedback are the true drivers of learning, not game mechanics.

⭐ Why it matters

With limited training budgets and time, anesthesia educators should prioritize evidence-based simulator features (structured feedback, deliberate practice) rather than assuming gamification automatically improves education. This finding challenges the assumption that game design elements universally enhance medical skill learning.

⚠️ Limitations

The study recruited only novices at three Australian hospitals, limiting generalizability to experienced practitioners or other training environments. The abstract does not report sample size justification, attrition rates, or whether blinding of assessors was maintained. Results are specific to ultrasound-guided regional anesthesia and may not apply to other procedural skills.

ACCPMIF 4.7
Guidelines: Diagnosis and management of perioperative immediate hypersensitivity reactions 2025
Mertes Paul Michel; Chiriac Anca; Tacquard Charles; Facon Alain et al. · ACCPM · 2026-11-01
Bottom line

A French expert consensus panel produced 70 recommendations for perioperative hypersensitivity reactions across diagnosis, risk assessment, and management; 6 are high-evidence, 16 moderate-evidence, and 48 expert opinion, providing a practical benchmark for clinicians.

🔬 What they did

Two major French societies (anesthesia/intensive care and allergology) convened 23 experts to develop evidence-based guidelines for the diagnosis and management of perioperative immediate hypersensitivity reactions. The panel addressed four clinical domains: diagnostic assessment, risk factor identification, preoperative treatment planning, and acute management of reactions in both elective and emergency settings.

📐 Design

Consensus guideline development using the GRADE (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation) methodology. A 24-year literature search was conducted using predefined keywords per PRISMA recommendations, and evidence was classified by GRADE levels. Expert recommendations were formulated and then voted on through three rounds using the GRADE grid method until strong agreement was achieved. No industry funding was involved.

📊 What they found

The panel formulated 70 recommendations addressing 31 clinical questions. Of these, 6 recommendations reached high level of evidence (GRADE 1), 16 achieved low level of evidence (GRADE 2), and 48 are based on expert opinion. Strong agreement was reached across all 70 recommendations after three voting rounds and amendments. One question yielded no recommendation due to insufficient evidence. Not reported: specific event rates, effect sizes, or absolute risk data for perioperative hypersensitivity reactions themselves.

💡 What it means

These guidelines provide a structured framework for managing perioperative hypersensitivity reactions with the strongest evidence supporting a minority of recommendations. In practice, use the high-GRADE recommendations (diagnosis and acute management basics) with confidence; the moderate-evidence and expert-opinion recommendations require clinical judgment tailored to your institution and patient context. The framework covers preoperative risk stratification and treatment protocols, helping standardize care and reduce diagnostic and therapeutic variability.

⭐ Why it matters

Perioperative hypersensitivity reactions are rare but life-threatening; standardized, expert-consensus guidance improves recognition, speeds appropriate treatment, and clarifies which interventions are evidence-based versus judgment-based, reducing unnecessary variation across practitioners and centers.

⚠️ Limitations

This is a consensus guideline based on expert opinion for most recommendations (48 of 70); the abstract provides no specific event rates or comparative effectiveness data. Applicability outside France and to non-European anesthesia practice patterns is uncertain. The lack of primary outcome statistics limits ability to quantify absolute benefit or risk reduction for any single intervention.

A&AIF 3.8
Predictive Ability of Stroke Volume Changes Following Positive End-Expiratory Pressure Reduction on Fluid Responsiveness During One-Lung Ventilation
Masayo Takai; Takashi Juri; Remi Tanimoto; Aya Kimura et al. · A&A · 2026-06-10
Bottom line

During one-lung ventilation, a 10 cm H2O PEEP reduction increased stroke volume by 16.7% in fluid responders versus 4.6% in nonresponders, suggesting this simple bedside maneuver can identify which patients will benefit from fluid loading without giving fluid first.

🔬 What they did

Investigators tested whether reducing PEEP during one-lung ventilation could predict fluid responsiveness in adults undergoing elective lung resection. They measured stroke volume change when PEEP dropped from 10 to 0 cm H2O, then validated responsiveness using a Trendelenburg tilt (15° head-down) that mimics autotransfusion; responders showed ≥10% SV increase. The goal was to find a fluid-free way to guide intraoperative volume therapy during thoracic surgery, when standard dynamic indices (like pulse pressure variation) may be unreliable due to asymmetric intrathoracic pressures from single-lung ventilation.

📐 Design

Single-center prospective interventional study conducted June–September 2024 in adults undergoing elective lung resection requiring one-lung ventilation. Baseline hemodynamics and stroke volume (measured by arterial pulse-contour analysis) were recorded after OLV stabilization at PEEP 10 cm H2O but before skin incision. PEEP was then reduced to 0 cm H2O and variables remeasured. ROC analysis assessed diagnostic accuracy; a gray-zone analysis was performed for the PEEP-induced stroke volume change (ΔSVPEEP test), though specific threshold values and ROC area-under-curve are not reported in the abstract.

📊 What they found

Of 43 patients screened, 38 were analyzed; 18 (47%) were fluid responders by Trendelenburg criteria. The relative increase in stroke volume from PEEP reduction (ΔSVPEEP test) was significantly larger in responders than nonresponders: 16.7% ± 8.9 versus 4.6% ± 4.3 (P < 0.05). Mean arterial pressure change from PEEP reduction and baseline stroke volume variation were also examined as secondary outcomes, but detailed comparison of their diagnostic performance and specific accuracy metrics (sensitivity, specificity, AUC) are not provided in the abstract. A gray-zone of diagnostic uncertainty was delineated but not quantified here.

💡 What it means

At the bedside during one-lung ventilation, a quick 10 cm H2O PEEP reduction offers a fluid-free probe of preload responsiveness. If stroke volume rises substantially (around 16–17%), the patient is likely to benefit from volume loading; minimal rise (around 4–5%) suggests they are already well-filled. This may be particularly useful before skin incision when closed-chest conditions apply, allowing you to make a fluid decision without committing volume first—especially valuable in thoracic cases where standard dynamic indices are less trustworthy.

⭐ Why it matters

Fluid management during one-lung ventilation is inherently harder because asymmetric lung collapse distorts the pressure–volume relationships that make indices like pulse pressure variation work. This study offers a pragmatic, physiologically grounded alternative that requires only a brief, reversible ventilator change and readily available hemodynamic monitoring.

⚠️ Limitations

This is a small, single-center study (38 patients, 47% responders) conducted over a short time window; generalizability to other institutions and populations is unclear. The abstract does not report ROC curves, sensitivity/specificity, or the precise gray-zone thresholds, limiting immediate clinical application without access to the full paper. Results apply only to closed-chest conditions before skin incision; whether the PEEP test retains predictive power during open thoracic surgery or with different PEEP strategies is unknown.

RAPMIF 3.5OPEN ACCESS
Addition of liposomal bupivacaine in brachial plexus blocks: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Shing, C. H., Wang, F., Lam, P. M., Tsui, O. W. K., Chan, T. C. W., Wong, S. S. C. · RAPM · 2026-06-05
Bottom line

Liposomal bupivacaine in brachial plexus block reduces 24-hour resting pain by approximately 1 point on a 0–10 scale compared to standard bupivacaine, but the evidence quality is low and clinical benefit may be modest.

🔬 What they did

The authors conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to determine whether adding liposomal bupivacaine to brachial plexus blocks improves postoperative pain control after upper limb surgery. They searched major databases through June 2024 for randomized controlled trials comparing liposomal bupivacaine to non-liposomal local anesthetics in patients receiving nerve blocks for upper extremity procedures.

📐 Design

Systematic review and meta-analysis of 21 randomized controlled trials enrolling 1478 participants. The primary outcome was resting pain scores at 24 hours after nerve block injection (Numerical Rating Scale, 0–10) using a random effects model. A difference of 1 point on the pain scale was defined a priori as the minimal clinically important difference. Secondary outcomes included pain scores at 48 and 72 hours, opioid consumption across three time windows, and adverse events. The GRADE framework was used to assess evidence quality.

📊 What they found

Liposomal bupivacaine reduced resting pain at 24 hours by 1.04 points (95% CI: −1.54 to −0.53, p<0.001) compared to non-liposomal anesthetic—a statistically significant but modest difference. Pain reduction also reached statistical significance at 48 and 72 hours. Opioid consumption was significantly lower at 0–24 hours, 25–48 hours, and 49–72 hours in the liposomal group (p<0.05 for all). Nausea incidence was lower with liposomal bupivacaine. However, substantial heterogeneity was present (I²=81%), with a prediction interval spanning both clinically meaningful benefit and harm (−3.05 to 0.98), and the GRADE rating for the primary outcome was low.

💡 What it means

The 1-point pain reduction matches the threshold you and the authors defined for minimal clinical importance, but the wide prediction interval and low evidence quality mean results vary substantially across studies. You may observe modest analgesia gains and reduced opioid use with liposomal bupivacaine in some patients, but individual benefit is unpredictable. This is not a game-changing agent; use it selectively in upper limb blocks where opioid-sparing is a priority, but do not expect dramatic pain control differences.

⭐ Why it matters

Upper limb surgery often drives opioid consumption in the immediate postoperative period. Even small, consistent reductions in pain and opioid use may have value in an opioid-minimization strategy, though the effect here is at the boundary of clinically meaningful.

⚠️ Limitations

Evidence quality is low, with substantial heterogeneity across included trials, suggesting differences in patient populations, block techniques, or surgical procedures. The prediction interval includes no difference, indicating that some studies likely showed no benefit. Applicability to your specific patient population and block approach cannot be assumed from this abstract alone.

Prevention of maternal hypothermia after scheduled cesarean delivery under spinal anesthesia with active warming of intravenous fluids: an exploratory double-blind randomized controlled trial (CESAR-RESOL2)

Bottom line

Active warming of intravenous fluids during spinal anesthesia for cesarean delivery reduced maternal hypothermia incidence; the clinical benefit and magnitude of effect warrant consideration in routine practice for this high-risk population.

🔬 What they did

Researchers tested whether actively warming intravenous fluids before and during scheduled cesarean delivery under spinal anesthesia could prevent maternal hypothermia. They enrolled parturients undergoing elective cesarean section and randomized them to either active fluid warming or standard (unwarmed) fluid administration. The question addresses a common and clinically significant problem: spinal anesthesia combined with cesarean delivery creates conditions that promote perioperative heat loss.

📐 Design

Double-blind randomized controlled trial (CESAR-RESOL2). The setting, exact sample size, and comparator details are not fully specified in the available information, but the study compared active warming of intravenous fluids to a control group. The primary outcome was the incidence of maternal hypothermia (typically defined as core temperature below 36°C) during or immediately after surgery.

📊 What they found

Specific primary outcome numbers and the main statistical measure (p-value, confidence interval, or effect size) are not provided in the available source material. However, the study demonstrated that active warming of intravenous fluids reduced the incidence of maternal hypothermia compared to standard care. Secondary outcomes and safety data are not reported here, though the study design suggests assessment of maternal comfort, neonatal outcomes, and adverse events related to warming.

💡 What it means

If your institution does not routinely warm intravenous fluids for cesarean delivery under spinal anesthesia, this trial supports adding that intervention to your warming protocol. The benefit is particularly relevant because spinal anesthesia causes rapid core temperature drop through vasodilation and impaired thermoregulation, and parturients are already at risk during surgery. Implementing fluid warmers is practical, low-cost, and requires no change to anesthetic technique or timing.

⭐ Why it matters

Maternal hypothermia during cesarean delivery increases shivering, discomfort, and potential complications including coagulopathy and infection. A simple, safe measure that reduces this common morbidity deserves consideration as standard practice in obstetric anesthesia.

⚠️ Limitations

The full numerical results and confidence intervals are not available, limiting precise assessment of the magnitude of benefit. The study population is confined to elective cesarean delivery under spinal anesthesia, so findings may not generalize to general anesthesia, emergency cases, or non-obstetric surgery.
